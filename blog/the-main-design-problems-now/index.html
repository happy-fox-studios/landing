Main problems of the current design

At first look it is hard to distinguish a boss from a regular mob. In such cases, there are several reasons that create a general perception:
  • Level of detailing:
    the simplest mob has more elements and a silhouette more complex than the boss.
  • Quantity (quality in some cases) of colors/ materials:
    the simplest mob has a wider palette of colours and a wider range of materials.
  • Size compared with that of the main character and one another:
    during the game our brain analyzes the image and mentally correlates it with items of the known size. Therefore, the viewer perceives everything based on the size of the main character.

The size indicator for humanoid creatures is usually the size of the head. While it is acceptable to make the bosses' heads bigger than the main character’s in order to visually show the strength of the mobs, it should not be done for simpler mobs. Otherwise, such feature will create a constant dissonance of "expectations/reality" in the viewer’s head.

It can be a cool thing to create a connection between the mobs and the game lore. Right now mobs are perceived as separate characters: there isn’t a single visual style in the design. As a result, many mobs are not connected with each other visually so it is not clear how and under what conditions they can exist in a single location. There are two factors at play here:

  • inconsistencies in style: a neat-looking ram vs. a scruffy-looking draugr, or well crafted design of a plant with a complex silhouette vs. a plain bug.
  • theme inconsistencies: a mutant rat vs. a werewolf zombie in Greek armor (?).

The player may have a problem understanding the type of attack of certain mobs: it can be hard to predict their behavior and what danger the main character can face.
Typical examples.
  • Rat boss. Currently, the zones that pose a danger to the player are poorly read. You can assume from the spiked bands that the Rat will attack with its tail. However, due to the lack of visual emphasis on this part of the body, the player will most likely not pay attention to it. The contrast in colours, sizes, or a significant silhouette of the object can serve as a visual emphasis.

  • Flies of close and long-range combat. These mobs are very similar in color and silhouette, so the player will not be able to tell right away which fly uses which type of attack.

  • Plants of close and long-range combat. These mobs are identified as stationary, so they are well read visually. The close combat plant is represented as a static barrier in the form of a thorned wall which the player will have to cut with a weapon — if that was the idea, it’s alright.

However, it is less clear with the plant of long-range combat. If it’s going to be static like the other type of plant and mounted on the walls, we will need to work out the position of the leaves: they are currently located as if they were under the vertical force of gravity. If that’s not the case, the artist must put more detail into the mechanics of weapon release and the movements of the plants' "barrels", to put the design emphases in such a way that the player would see and recognize danger from a long distance.

Character design approach.
Since the game is quite lengthy and has different locations, it makes sense to break the enemies into several different sets and work out the hierarchy of mobs within them. Each set of characters will be tied to a location by theme, colour, and lore. At the same time, you can have a certain set of standard weak mobs that will be found in many (or all) locations — you can also slightly change their colour or design in accordance with the location, while leaving them recognizable. In Hollow Knight, these mobs are the Crawlid and the flying Vengefly.

Before developing characters, it is important to determine the key features of the locations, and ideally, prepare fake shots of at least a small section of each location. That way, when designing characters, it will be possible to simply imagine them on the spot and identify the weaknesses of the design. Here it’s necessary to pay attention to how the characters fit into the style of the location as well as the image of the character placed before the background.

To determine the colour scheme of the mob sets, it will also be useful to set the colour map of the game with the intended final colour solutions for the locations.

Here's how we might approach the concept of the undead set of characters.
There’s a lone necromancer who resides in the dungeon. He conducts experiments to create the undead. The undead are under the wizard’s control, and the design reflects it through the green color of the magic from the boss' staff which in turn correlates with the green colour of the undead’s eyes. Here’s an example of creating a visual connection with the mobs through lore — the magic staff has a goat skull at the base, and that justifies the presence of the goat/ram in the dungeons. You can also create the connection by linking design and gameplay: the boss will call on the undead with the glowing green eyes during the battle, but as soon as the player destroys the necromancer’s staff, the eyes of the undead will die out, and the mobs will fall to pieces.

The more links you create between the design, the lore and the gameplay, the more elaborate and captivating the game process will be.

Connection with surroundings.
It's crucial for the characters to be connected with the location they're present at as well as with each other. This is necessary to make them create a single image for the player.

The connection can be achieved through colour, association, and objects. For instance, the same swords and the old armor that the undead mobs use can lie here and there in the dungeons — in the storage room and catacombs — and we can also see skulls and bones at the location — the deeper into the dungeon you go, the more of them you come across. It would also be a good thing to work on the location of the battle with the boss and on the concept of the boss himself simultaneously. For example, the battle with the necromancer can take place in the crypt under the castle that he turned into his lab. The closer the player gets to the boss' location, the more objects associated with him he'll see.

Final graphics processing
Basically, we have already mentioned the aspects of character graphics processing above. However, when doing the final round, it's important to take into account the following factors, even if they seem less significant sometimes:


  1. When it comes to line graphics, we have to keep an eye on the line thickness so that when we view the characters 1:1, they are perceived by the player as of the same size.

  2. It's better to regulate specific style features, like shadows or lack thereof, their directions and depth. What I mean is for instance, all characters should be lit from above, and the shadows should be cast through the multiply mode with the colour black and 30% transparency.

  3. I would also pay attention to the position of characters' feet. Right now the main character and the werewolf-boss' feet are drawn in a way that makes it look like they're standing on the 2.5D ground, while the other mobs — draugr, ram, the necromancer boss — are positioned on 2D.

It's a good thing to put all the characters in one line for checkup. Artist to artist, I would recommend doing the final graphics processing when you see all the characters together in order to understand how the mobs are perceived next to each other. I've attached the examples of putting them in one line below: the first two lines were put up from the current materials, and the third line is my idea of approximate sizes of the mobs in the game.
